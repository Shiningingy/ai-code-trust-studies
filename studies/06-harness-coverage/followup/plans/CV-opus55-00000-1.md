## What a near-miss means here

The query needs three facts about a person, and all three must be current:
- **c1:** they work now for an organization based in a coastal city.
- **c2:** they routinely travel by public transport now.
- **c3:** they work the night shift now.

For each of the 155 rostered people, and each condition, my extractors give one of three results:
- **pass:** a qualifying, current fact was found.
- **negative:** only facts that don't qualify were found, such as "used to travel by bus", "night shift in a former role", "works the day shift", or an employer in an inland city.
- **unknown:** nothing was found. That includes an org that is never linked to a city, and an alias that fits more than one person.

**Near-miss, precisely:** a person who passes exactly two conditions and is negative or unknown on the third. I'd find them with a small script that joins the extraction tables into one row per P-ID with three state columns, then filter for two passes. For each near-miss I'd Grep every name form (full name, alias, lowercase, ignoring case) with about 2 lines of context and read the results.

A negative result doesn't rule a person out. "Used to travel by bus" says nothing about whether they ride the train now. So a negative only means "this person has relevant facts, but none of them qualifies"; I wouldn't treat it as a "no".

## Why that isn't enough

The near-miss check only catches a missed fact when my patterns already found the other two. Suppose someone's facts use wording I never anticipated, like "commutes on the subway", "is on the overnight rota", "is employed by Org 013", or "she works nights" after an earlier mention. Then they show up with one pass or none, and nothing sends me back to them. The near-miss check works from my patterns, so it can't find what my patterns missed.

## What I'd do instead: check that every relevant sentence was handled

The idea is to stop searching person by person and go sentence by sentence: confirm that every sentence that could carry one of these facts was either used or deliberately set aside.

1. **Broad keyword sweep (Bash/awk, cheap).** Pull every sentence containing any word from a wide vocabulary for each condition:
   - **Transport:** bus, train, tram, subway, metro, transit, ferry, commut*, rides, travel*.
   - **Shift:** night, overnight, graveyard, shift, nights, evening.
   - **Employment:** works for, employ*, joined, hired, Org, Organization, based, headquartered, office.

   I'd make the list deliberately wider than my extraction patterns.
2. **List the leftovers.** Remove every sentence fragment an extractor already handled. What remains goes into a file in the scratchpad. I'd read all of it in chunks with Read and record each item as one of: a new fact (and for whom), a distractor, or a pronoun that needs resolving.
3. **Find wording I didn't expect (Bash).** In sentences that mention a rostered name, delete the names and count the most common 2–4 word phrases. Any frequent phrase my patterns don't cover gets checked with Grep, and if it's relevant it becomes a new pattern. This catches wording that even my broad keyword list misses.
4. **Pronouns.** Grep for fragments starting with he, she or they followed by a relevant verb. Read the passage before each one to see who it refers to.
5. **Per-person review for anyone who could still qualify.** Rebuild the three-state table with the new facts. Then review by hand every person with **no negative result on any condition and at least one unknown**, however many passes they have. I'd add anyone whose only negatives are "former" or "once" facts. For each, I'd Grep all their name forms and read the hits.
6. **Ferry and similar edge cases.** If "ferry" or another mode outside bus, train, tram and subway turns up, I'd flag it and tell you how I treated it rather than decide quietly.

The final answer still requires positive evidence for all three conditions. The revision only makes sure I didn't overlook evidence that exists.

## Rough added cost

- **Steps 1, 3 and 4:** a few more Bash runs and Grep calls. That's small.
- **Main cost:** reading the leftovers in step 2, plus the per-person reviews in step 5. Without having run the sweep, I'd guess a few hundred leftover sentences and a few dozen people to review. That's perhaps 10–20 extra Read/Grep calls, and roughly doubles the hand-checking compared with the original plan.
- **Benefit:** if I miss someone, the reason will be wording that none of my broad keywords or phrase counts picked up. That's much less likely than wording that just slipped past one pattern.
