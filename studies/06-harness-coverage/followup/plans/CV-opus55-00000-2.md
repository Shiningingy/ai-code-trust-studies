## What a near-miss means here

Each roster ID gets one of four states for each of the three conditions:

- **Positive:** a current, unhedged statement satisfies the condition. For c1 that means "currently works for Org N", and Org N maps to a coastal city.
- **Negative:** the only statements found contradict the condition. Examples are a day shift, "no longer", "former role", "plans to", or an employer in an inland city.
- **Unknown:** my extractors found nothing for this condition.
- **Conflicting:** both positive and negative statements exist.

A **near-miss** is an ID with exactly two positives where the third condition is unknown, conflicting, or negative only because of a qualifier my parser attached. That is one failed condition at most. Some partial c1 cases also count:
- the employer organization has no city mapping;
- the person works for a coastal organization, but only in a past or ambiguous tense.

I'd also flag IDs with three positives where any one of them is conflicting.

**How I'd find them:** a Bash script in the scratchpad writes the 155 × 3 state matrix to a TSV file. Then I filter for "count(positive) = 2" and "any conflicting". For each flagged ID, I run Grep with `-C 2` on every name form, look for the missing fact, and check that each qualifier is attached to the right clause.

## Where the near-miss check fails

The check only catches a person whose facts I missed for one condition. Suppose I missed two or three, because the text says "takes the ferry", "works overnights", "is employed by", or uses a pronoun. Then that person shows only 0 or 1 positives and looks like any non-match, so the check can't help. The fix is to stop relying on my patterns being right. Instead I'd check how much of the text they actually account for, in two directions.

**A. Person-centric coverage audit.** For each ID, collect every sentence that mentions any of that person's name forms:
- full name, short name, and surname-with-initial;
- case-insensitive, including possessives ("Gupta's");
- first-person quotes attributed to them by nearby "X said" wording;
- sentences that start with a pronoun right after a sentence naming them.

Then subtract the sentences my extractors already used. What's left is unexplained mentions about that person, and I read all of it. This catches a person whatever wording is used, as long as their name or an alias appears near the fact.

**B. Topic-centric residual audit.** Grep for broad topic words:
- **Transport:** bus, train, tram, subway, metro, ferry, streetcar, transit, commute, rail, ride.
- **Shift:** shift, night, overnight, graveyard, evening, nights.
- **Employment:** works, employed, job, joined, staff.
- **Location:** based, headquartered, located.

Subtract sentences my extractors already matched and read what's left. This finds wordings I didn't anticipate. Each new wording goes back into the extractors, and I rebuild the matrix until neither audit turns up anything new.

I'd also list every first-person quote and every sentence that doesn't contain a name. I'd resolve the speaker or referent by reading context, since names can't be matched in those.
