## What a near-miss means here

A near-miss is a person the strict extraction rejects, but only narrowly, so that one missed or misread fact could flip the result. For each person and each condition I'd record one of three states:

- **Pass:** a matching fact attributed to them.
- **Counter-evidence:** an explicit disqualifier, such as "no longer takes the bus", a past employer, only `old-event`s, or a temporary dairy skip.
- **No evidence:** nothing found either way.

A person counts as a near-miss if any of these holds:
1. **Three passes and one "no evidence".** This is the main case. Silence is what a missed pattern looks like.
2. **Three passes and one counter-evidence.** The disqualifier may be attached to the wrong person, or a later sentence may reverse it. These are worth checking, but less likely to flip.
3. **Four passes, one of them weak.** For example: c2 at exactly 3 events where one could be a duplicate or a case variant, an employer linked through an ambiguous alias, or a city whose coastal status I judged myself.
4. **Three passes, with c2 at exactly 2 distinct events.** One missed volunteering sentence would push them over the threshold.

**How I'd find them:** the extraction script writes a table with one row per roster ID and four columns. Each column holds the state plus the supporting sentence numbers. A filter then lists people with at least 3 passes, or 2 passes plus c2 = 2. For each one I'd Grep every name form and Read those passages in full.

## When the near-miss check isn't enough

Suppose a real match has facts my patterns miss for two or more conditions. For example, their transport sentence uses "commutes on the ferry" and their dairy sentence is a quote attributed through a lowercase alias. They'd have only 2 passes and never appear as a near-miss. Checking around candidates can't catch this, because the person was never a candidate.

So I'd stop relying on my patterns being complete and check coverage of the text itself, in three ways:

**A. Template inventory (the main fix).** The passages look generated from a limited set of sentence templates. A script would:
- replace every resolved name, alias, `P-###`, `Organization NNN`, event ID and city with placeholders;
- split on connectors ("while", "but", "and", "In a separate update,");
- count the distinct sentence shapes that result.

I'd expect somewhere around 50–200 shapes. I'd Read one example of each, plus frequency counts, and label each shape as satisfying c1–c4, disqualifying c1–c4, or irrelevant. Extraction then runs from that labelled table instead of from regexes I guessed in advance. Any phrasing used anywhere in the file has been seen at least once. This also turns up name forms I missed, because they won't be replaced and will stand out in the shapes.

**B. Broad keyword nets per condition, then review what's left.** For each condition, a deliberately loose keyword list:
- **c1:** organization, works, employ, job, joined, hired
- **c2:** volunteer, event-, helped at, staffed
- **c3:** dairy, milk, cheese, butter, yogurt, cream, lactose, vegan
- **c4:** bus, train, tram, metro, subway, ferry, transit, commute, ride

Any sentence a net catches but the strict extraction didn't classify gets printed for review. I'd add rules and rerun until every leftover sentence is either classified or confirmed irrelevant.

**C. Attribution audit.** Two checks:
- List every sentence that hits a keyword net but resolved to no person, or to more than one person without being split. These are the pronoun, quote, unknown-alias and lowercase cases.
- List capitalized or lowercase name-like tokens that aren't in the alias map.

Both lists get fixed or ruled on before anything is intersected.

Once A–C have left no unexplained sentences, I'd rerun the near-miss check from the previous section. At that point it really is catching the single remaining mistake, not hiding several.
