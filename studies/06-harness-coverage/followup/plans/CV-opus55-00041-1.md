# Near-misses, and what to do when a near-miss check isn't enough

## 1. What a near-miss means here

A near-miss check is a way to find out where my patterns failed. The idea is that a qualifying person whose fact was missed for one condition shows up as passing four of five. To make that precise, each person gets a status for each condition:

- **PASS:** a positive pattern matched. For c1, that means three or more distinct this-year events.
- **FAIL-DECOY:** only look-alike patterns matched, such as emergency contact only, last-year events only, train chosen because of a disruption, "skipping cheese", or a disliked fruit.
- **UNKNOWN:** nothing about this condition was found for this person.
- **PARTIAL:** only for c1, meaning exactly one or two distinct this-year events.

**A near-miss** is someone with four PASS results whose fifth condition is UNKNOWN, PARTIAL or FAIL-DECOY. For c2, the check covers both links in the chain: either the person has no guardian record, or they are a legal guardian but the youth's current evening course wasn't found. The second case is a miss on the youth's side, not the person's.

**How I'd find them:** a script run through Bash builds a table of people against conditions with these statuses, then filters it. For each near-miss, I'd grep every alias of that person, and the youth's ID for c2, with a few lines of context, then use Read to check whether the missing fact is there in a form my patterns didn't recognise.

## 2. Why that isn't enough

A near-miss check only catches failures that hit one condition. If my patterns miss a wording that appears for several of someone's conditions, they come out at three of five or lower and look like a clear non-match. The same thing happens if a whole passage format is missed, such as a long sentence joined by commas or quoted speech. It also happens if an alias doesn't resolve, which removes a whole group of that person's facts at once. Lowering the threshold to three of five only pushes the problem back a step. It doesn't fix it.

So instead of asking which people are close to qualifying, I'd ask a different question: **which parts of the text did my patterns fail to explain?** That check doesn't depend on how many conditions a miss affects.

## 3. Revised plan

Steps 1–4 stay as they were: the name list, name normalisation, splitting sentences into facts, and checking each condition. The changes are:

**5. Account for every clause (new; this is the main recall check).**
The Bash script tags every clause in all 2,327 passages as one of:
- (a) a positive fact,
- (b) a known look-alike,
- (c) known filler, using strict patterns so real facts can't hide in this bucket, or
- (d) **unexplained.**

I then go through every unexplained clause with Grep and Read. Each new wording I find becomes a pattern, and I run the script again until (d) holds nothing related to any condition. This catches misses no matter how many of a person's conditions they affect.

**6. Account for every name (expanded from step 2).**
The script pulls every name-like phrase: capitalised words, initial plus surname, lowercase first name plus surname, and names inside quotes. It then reports any that don't map to a P-ID, and any P-ID with surprisingly few mentions. Each one gets resolved by hand. This targets the alias problem, which is the failure most likely to remove several conditions at once.

**7. Run a second, looser extractor and compare.**
A second script matches loose keywords (`event-`, `guardian`, `Y\d+|Youth \d+`, `train`, any fruit name, `dairy|milk|cheese|butter`, `evening_`) and gives each hit to the nearest resolved name. Any case where the strict and loose versions disagree about a person and condition gets reviewed. The two methods fail in different ways, so cases that both miss should be rare.

**8. Near-miss check (as in section 1)**, plus a review of anyone at **three of five** whose failing conditions are UNKNOWN rather than FAIL-DECOY. Having nothing found is a weaker sign than having only a look-alike found.

**9. Look for later statements that cancel earlier ones** (the old step 5), using Grep for "no longer", "used to", "stopped", "anymore" and similar.

**10. Verify each final candidate by hand**, then write the sorted `{"matches": [...]}`.

## 4. Extra cost

- **My earlier plan:** roughly 15–25 tool calls.
- **The revision:** roughly 15–30 more. Most of that is step 5, which depends on how many clauses come out unexplained. A few hundred is likely, reviewed in grouped batches through Grep rather than read one by one. Steps 6 and 7 are cheap script runs plus a short review list.
- **Overall:** about 1.5 to 2 times the work and context.

What it buys: missed facts stop depending on guesswork about near-misses and get checked as part of accounting for the whole text. The remaining risk is wording that a filler pattern classes as filler by mistake. That's why I'd keep the filler patterns narrow and spot-check a sample of filler clauses with Read.
