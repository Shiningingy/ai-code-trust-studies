## What a near-miss is here

For each person and each condition (c1 coastal-city employer, c2 public transport, c3 current night shift), I'd record one of three statuses:

- **confirmed**: a present-tense passage supports it.
- **contradicted**: a passage says otherwise, such as an inland employer, a car commute, or a day shift. A later passage can supersede an earlier one.
- **unknown**: I found no passage either way.

A **near-miss** is a person with exactly two conditions confirmed and the third **unknown**. That person might be a real match whose third fact my patterns missed. People with a **contradicted** condition are real failures, unless a later passage corrects the contradiction. I'd keep them as a separate list and re-check the correction case.

I'd find near-misses in three steps:

1. Run a Python script through Bash that joins the tables and lists everyone with at least two confirmed conditions.
2. For each person, run Grep on every alias from the roster passages, using a wider vocabulary for the missing condition. That means words like "overnight", "graveyard", "rides", "commutes" and "tram", plus any other phrasing I sample from the file.
3. Read the passages that turn up.

## Why that isn't enough

The near-miss check only recovers a person whose patterns caught two of the three facts. If my patterns miss several of a person's facts, that person has zero or one confirmed condition. They never reach the near-miss list, and I'd drop them without knowing. Widening the vocabulary doesn't fix this, because the unseen phrasings are the ones I haven't thought of.

## Revised plan: read per person instead of searching by pattern

1. **Build the roster first.** The ID-to-name passages are structured ("identifier P-036 and also uses the familiar names…"). I'd extract them with Grep and a script into a scratchpad table of ID, canonical name and aliases. This step doesn't depend on guessing condition vocabulary.
2. **Strip the noise by template, not by keyword.** A script in Bash/Python would replace names, numbers and nouns in each passage with placeholders and count how often each template appears. Filler templates ("owns a telescope", "recommended a book") will repeat many times. Rare templates, and any passage that mentions work, travel, schedule, organizations or cities, would be kept. I'd check a sample of the discarded templates by eye to confirm they're truly irrelevant.
3. **Build a dossier per person.** For each ID, a script would collect every kept passage that mentions any of their aliases and write them to a file in the scratchpad. I'd mark surname-only or initial-only matches as ambiguous so they aren't attached to the wrong ID.
4. **Collect organization and city facts separately.** Employer-to-city and city-to-coastal passages may not mention people at all. I'd gather every passage that names an organization or a city, using the organization and city names I find in the dossiers, and build those two lookup tables from them.
5. **Read the dossiers and the org/city passages with Read.** I'd assign each condition a status (confirmed, contradicted or unknown) with tense, negation and later corrections in mind. Every person gets all three statuses from reading their full dossier, not from patterns.
6. **Run the near-miss check as a cross-check.** The keyword near-miss list from the original plan becomes a consistency test. Anyone it flags who my dossier reading excluded gets a second look.
7. **Intersect, verify and format.** I'd keep only people with all three conditions confirmed, re-read their supporting passages, sort the IDs with a plain string sort, and output the JSON object.

## What the revision adds in cost

- **Tokens:** the keyword plan reads perhaps 10–20% of the file, around 20–30k tokens. The dossier plan reads most of what survives template filtering, plus the org/city passages. I'd estimate 60–150k tokens, roughly 3–5 times as much. In the worst case, where filtering removes little, it means reading the whole file.
- **Tool calls:** the file is 9,804 lines, so a full read takes about 25 Read calls of roughly 400 lines each. The scripts, the sample checks and the dossier reads add perhaps 15–30 calls on top.
- **Risk reduced:** this removes the dependence on patterns for finding facts. The remaining risk is ambiguous names, which I'd handle by only linking a fact to an ID when the passage uses an alias the roster states explicitly.
