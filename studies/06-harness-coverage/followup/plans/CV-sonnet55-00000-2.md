## What a near-miss means here

A near-miss is a person who satisfies exactly two of c1, c2 and c3 under my extraction, and for whom the third is missing or unresolved. The check is meant to catch cases where my patterns failed on one fact.

I would define it precisely as follows:
- **Tiers.** Tier 1 is people with 2 of 3 conditions confirmed. Each one gets a read of the raw passages for the missing condition. Tier 2 is people with 1 of 3 confirmed. I'd only sample these, because there will be many of them.
- **What counts as a failed condition.** I'd separate "affirmatively false" (a stated day shift, an inland city, "does not take the bus") from "no evidence found". Only the second kind is a near-miss. The first kind is a real negative and I'd drop it.
- **How to find them.** The Python script would keep a per-ID table with three columns, each holding true, false or unknown, plus the source passage IDs. A Grep with `output_mode: content` and `-C` on the person's ID, names and aliases would pull every passage that mentions them. I'd read those passages for the missing condition.

## Why that isn't enough

The near-miss check assumes my patterns fail on at most one condition per person. If the wording is unusual for several conditions, a qualifying person may end up with only one condition found, or none. They would then sit in Tier 2 or below, where I'm only sampling. A structural miss can also hide a whole group. For example, if I don't recognise a phrasing for a city's coastal status, every employee of every organization in that city loses c1 at once.

## Revised plan

The change is to stop depending on the extraction patterns for recall and to make the evidence per person complete.

1. **Vocabulary pass, as before, but exhaustive.** I'd list every sentence template in the file, not just sample it. The passages are short and highly templated. The script would normalise each sentence by replacing names, IDs and org names with placeholders, then count the distinct templates. I'd read all of the templates, probably a few hundred, and classify each one as relevant to c1, c2, c3 or filler. This replaces guessing at synonyms with a complete list.
2. **Alias table, as before.** I'd build it from the identifier passages and flag any short name that maps to more than one ID.
3. **Per-person dossiers.** For every ID, the script would collect all passages that mention any of their names or aliases. It would also collect the passages about their organizations and those organizations' cities, following the chain person → org → city. It would write these to a file in the scratchpad.
4. **Classify with templates, then check with reading.** The template classification from step 1 gives the first-pass true, false or unknown for each condition. Every person with any unknown condition, or any condition resting on a template with a temporal qualifier or negation, gets their dossier read directly with Read. A dossier should be only a handful of short passages, so reading is feasible.
5. **Coastal list.** I'd list every distinct city in the file and decide coastal status for each one. I'd use any statement in the file first and my own knowledge otherwise, and I'd note which source I used.
6. **Cross-check.** I'd run a second independent pass using broad Grep terms for each condition, such as `night|overnight|graveyard|midnight` and `bus|train|metro|tram|subway|transit`. I'd compare the people it surfaces against the script's results and read the passages for any disagreement.
7. **Emit the JSON,** sorted lexicographically, after the final review.

## Added cost

- **Template enumeration:** a modest cost. It is one script run plus reading a few hundred normalised lines.
- **Dossier reading:** the main added cost. If about 10–20% of the several hundred people have an unknown or qualified condition, that is roughly 50–150 dossiers. I'd batch them into a few large Reads rather than reading one by one.
- **Cross-check Greps:** a few extra searches and a comparison step.

Overall, I'd expect roughly two to three times the effort of the original plan, mostly in reading. In return, recall no longer depends on my patterns being right for every condition.
