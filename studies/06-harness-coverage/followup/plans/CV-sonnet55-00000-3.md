## What a near-miss means here

A near-miss is a person who satisfies some of the three conditions (c1 coastal-city employer, c2 habitual public transport, c3 current night shift) but not all. I'd define it precisely as follows:

- **Strict near-miss:** the person has positive evidence for exactly two conditions, and the third is either contradicted or has no evidence.
- **Contradicted vs. unknown:** I'd keep these apart.
  - *Contradicted* means a passage says something like "day shift", "used to work nights", "plans to take the bus", or an employer in an inland city.
  - *Unknown* means I found no passage at all.
  - Unknown is the dangerous case. It may mean the fact is missing, or it may mean my patterns failed to find it.
- **Why it's useful:** near-misses are the people most likely to be real matches that I mis-scored. If someone has two conditions confirmed and the third is unknown, I go and look for the third fact specifically.

To find them, I'd build the per-ID tables from the plan, then list every ID with 2 of 3 conditions confirmed, plus every ID with 1 confirmed and 2 unknown. I'd read their passages directly with Read.

## Where that check fails

A near-miss check depends on my patterns catching at least two of a person's facts. Suppose a qualifying person has facts phrased in ways my patterns miss for several conditions, such as "graveyard hours", "rides the metro" or "headquartered in a harbor town". Then they score 0 or 1 and never show up as near-misses. They'd silently drop out. Looking harder at the near-miss list can't fix that, because the person isn't on the list.

## Revised plan

Stop relying on keyword patterns to decide who is a candidate. Instead, go person by person and read everything about each one.

1. **Build the identity layer first.** Use Grep to extract every roster-style passage (`identifier`, `familiar names`, `P-\d+`). Write a script in the scratchpad directory to produce a complete list of IDs with all their aliases and name variants, normalized for case, punctuation and initials. This gives the full population, which is probably a few hundred people at most.
2. **Gather every passage per person.** For each ID, the script collects all passages that mention any of its aliases. It does this with case-insensitive, whitespace-tolerant matching, not with topic keywords. It writes one dossier file per person to the scratchpad. Passages with no alias match go in a separate "unattributed" file.
3. **Learn the vocabulary from the data.** Before judging anything, I'd survey how facts are actually phrased. I'd count the distinct sentence templates in the corpus, with names and numbers masked, using the script. Because the passages look templated, there may be only a few dozen templates. I'd read the list and classify each template as relating to employer, city, transport, shift or filler. That replaces my guessed keyword list with the real one.
4. **Resolve the indirect links.** Extract the employer → city and city → coastal facts from the templates, as in the original plan. For cities with no stated coastal label, I'd note them separately and decide on them explicitly.
5. **Score from dossiers, not patterns.** Score each person per condition as confirmed, contradicted or unknown, using the learned templates. Any ID with an unknown condition gets its dossier read in full with Read, since dossiers are short.
6. **Check the unattributed file.** Read the passages that matched no alias, or a sample if the file is large. They may hold facts about people under a variant I didn't normalize.
7. **Output.** Return only the sorted JSON object.

## Added cost

- **Compute:** small. It's one more script and a pass over about 473 KB.
- **Reading:** this is the real addition. Reading template lists and the unknown-condition dossiers costs roughly tens of thousands of tokens, depending on how many people have unknowns. If the corpus has few templates, it's cheap. If it has hundreds, it's much more.
- **Benefit:** completeness no longer depends on my guessing the right words. Cost is bounded by the number of templates and unresolved people, not by the full 2,450 passages.
