## What a near-miss means here

A near-miss is a person who satisfies the query on the evidence I found, except for a small number of conditions. For this query I'd define it as follows:

- **Unit:** a resolved person ID, after merging aliases. It isn't a name string.
- **Status per condition:** each of c1–c4 is labeled one of three ways.
  - **Pass:** there is positive, current, unambiguous evidence.
  - **Fail:** there is explicit contrary evidence, such as "only in the past" or "does not ordinarily use public transport", or only 2 distinct this-year events.
  - **Unknown:** no evidence found, or the evidence is ambiguous.
- **Near-miss:** a person with exactly 3 Pass and 1 non-Pass. That non-Pass can be a Fail or an Unknown. I'd also include people with 2 Pass and 2 Unknown. Those are the people whose facts my patterns may have missed, so they matter more than the rest.
- **How I'd find them:** I'd build a per-person table of the four labels with the supporting passage IDs. Then I'd filter for the near-miss patterns and read the passages for each one. I'd look hardest at the Unknown cases. For those I'd search the file for the person's name variants and ID, and check whether a differently worded fact exists.

A near-miss check only works when the patterns catch most facts. It surfaces people who are one condition short. It can't surface someone whose facts my patterns missed for several conditions. That person would look like a 0- or 1-condition person and never be reviewed.

## What I'd do instead

I'd stop relying on pattern recall and make coverage a measured property. I'd do that with template normalization, which is independent of any particular pattern I guess.

1. **Normalize every passage into a template.** A Python script in the scratchpad (run via Bash) would read `context.md` and split it on `### Passage`. It would replace names, initials, IDs, event IDs, organization names, city names and years with placeholders such as `<NAME>`, `<PID>`, `<ORG>`, `<CITY>` and `<EVT>`. It would then count the distinct resulting templates. With about 2,370 passages from a generator, I expect only dozens to a few hundred templates. That is a guess, and the counts will confirm or refute it.

2. **Read every template once.** I'd use Read on the template list, plus one or two example passages for each. I'd label each template by what it asserts: which condition it bears on, its polarity (positive or negated), and its time scope (current or past). This replaces my guessed regexes with a complete inventory.

3. **Measure coverage.** Every passage then gets classified by its template label, and any passage left unlabeled counts as a failure. I'd keep going until zero passages are unlabeled. I'd also use Grep to cross-check, searching for keywords like `dairy`, `transport`, `volunteer` and `coastal` in passages my labels assigned to none of those conditions.

4. **Resolve identity on the full set.** Using the labeled ID-linking templates, I'd build the alias-to-ID map. Names that are ambiguous between IDs stay unresolved, and I'd report them as such.

5. **Compute the four conditions from the labeled facts.** The c1 organization and city tables come from labeled templates, as do the c2 event counts, the c3 diet facts and the c4 transport facts. Past, negated and last-year facts are handled by the label, not by regex luck.

6. **Verify with a sample, not only near-misses.** I'd print the full dossier for each candidate. I'd also read dossiers for a random sample of non-matches, plus every Unknown-heavy person. If a sampled non-match turns out to qualify, I'd treat that as a template-labeling bug and fix it.

## Added cost

- **Template step:** one script run, plus reading the template list. That's maybe tens of thousands of tokens, versus a few thousand for a regex-only pass.
- **Fallback:** if normalization leaves many unique templates, I'd read the file in full with Read in chunks. At about 469 KB that is roughly 120k+ tokens. That is the most expensive case, but it removes the dependence on my patterns.
- **Overall:** the revision costs roughly 3–5× the original plan in reading and tool calls, but the coverage is measured instead of assumed.
