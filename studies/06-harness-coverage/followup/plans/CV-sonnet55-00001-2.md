## What a near-miss means here

A near-miss is a person who satisfies most of the four conditions but not all. I would define it by the number of conditions failed:

- **Near-miss, distance 1:** the person passes exactly 3 of the 4 conditions. This is the main check. For each condition, I list who passes the other three and fails only that one. Then I read their raw passages for the failed condition.
- **Distance 2:** the person passes exactly 2 of the 4 conditions. I'd use this only if the distance-1 list shows that my patterns are weak.

The point of the check is to catch false negatives. A person who truly qualifies, but whose fact I failed to extract for one condition, will look like a distance-1 near-miss. I find them by reading the raw text for that single failed condition. That means searching by the person's ID and every alias, not only by my regexes.

I'd also look at people who pass all four, but only barely. For c2, that is someone with exactly 2 or exactly 3 distinct events, depending on the query's threshold. I'd also look at someone whose c3 or c4 status rests on one passage with unusual wording. These are likely false positives.

## Where the near-miss check fails

It fails when my patterns miss facts for several conditions of the same person. Such a person might pass only 1 or 2 conditions in my extraction, so they land at distance 2 or 3 and never show up. A systematic gap, such as an unrecognised phrasing for public transport used throughout, would hide many people this way. Widening the near-miss distance doesn't fix that well. It buries me in candidates and still relies on the same patterns.

## Revised plan

I would stop depending on my patterns alone. I'd make the extraction cover the whole text, and check it against what's actually in the file.

1. **Profile the phrasings first.** Use Grep and a Bash Python script to cluster the sentence templates in `context.md`. I'd strip IDs, names, numbers and cities from each passage, then count the distinct templates. The roughly 2,370 passages probably come from a small set of templates plus noise. I'd read every template once, with an example. This replaces guessing at phrasings with a full list.
2. **Classify every passage.** Each passage gets a type: roster, employment, org-city, volunteering, diet, commute, or other. I'd then read all the "other" passages, or a large sample, to confirm that nothing relevant is in there. The goal is that every passage is either accounted for or deliberately set aside.
3. **Resolve names, as before.** Build the alias table from the roster passages. Flag ambiguous short forms and don't attribute facts through them without more evidence.
4. **Extract per condition with the full template list.** Keep the matched passage numbers with each fact, so every result can be traced.
5. **Coverage audit.** For each person, count the passages that mention them by ID or any alias, and compare that with the number of passages my extraction explained. Passages that mention a person but support no extracted fact are the places where a missed fact would be. I'd read these for anyone who is close to qualifying. This is the step that catches several conditions missed at once, because it doesn't rely on condition counts.
6. **Intersect, then check with near-misses.** Take the intersection of the four sets and sort it as strings. Then run the distance-1 check, and the distance-2 check if the audit showed weaknesses. Read the raw text for the candidates and the near-misses.
7. **Check the edge cases.** Include the temporal and negation traps from before, the coastal-city question, and the string sort of IDs.

The coverage audit adds one more script and a targeted read of the flagged passages.
