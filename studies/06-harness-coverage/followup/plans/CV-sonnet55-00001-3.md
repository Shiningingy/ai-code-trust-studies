## What a near-miss means here

A near-miss is a person who my pipeline says satisfies **exactly 3 of the 4 conditions**, so they fail one. I'd define it precisely:

- **Pipeline status per condition:** each person gets one of three values for each condition: `yes`, `no` (an explicit contrary fact was found), or `unknown` (no fact found).
- **Near-miss, strict:** exactly three conditions are `yes` and the fourth is `no` or `unknown`.
- **Near-miss, loose:** three or more conditions are `yes` or `unknown`, with at most one `no`. This catches people whose missing facts my patterns didn't find.

I'd review the `unknown` cases first. If the one failing condition is `no` because of an explicit contrary statement like "only in the past", the exclusion is probably right. If it is `unknown`, the likeliest explanation is that my patterns missed a phrasing, an alias, or a hop in the c1 chain.

To find them, I'd use the per-person status table from the parse step. I'd list everyone with at most one non-`yes` condition, then use Grep on each person's ID and aliases to read their raw passages. I'd check whether the missing fact is actually stated in a form my patterns didn't recognize. If it is, I'd add the pattern and re-run the parse.

## Where the near-miss check fails

The check assumes my patterns miss at most one fact per qualifying person. Suppose they miss facts for **two or more conditions** of the same person. That person shows only two `yes` values and looks like a clear non-match. Neither the near-miss list nor a three-of-four filter would surface them. The cause is a systematic gap in vocabulary or aliasing. Lowering the threshold to "two of four" would only bring in a lot of noise.

## What I'd do instead

I'd stop relying on my patterns as the filter. Instead I'd make the **pattern-free** parts of the data complete, then read the raw text for candidates. The revision has four parts.

1. **Build a person-to-passages index that doesn't depend on fact patterns.** Using the roster passages, I'd collect every ID and alias, including initial-plus-surname forms. Then I'd script a scan of all ~2,300 passages. It would attach each passage to every person whose name, alias, or ID appears in it, and write the result to the scratchpad. This uses only names, which are far more regular than the fact phrasings. Ambiguous aliases would attach to all possible people and be flagged.

2. **Measure pattern coverage and not just precision.** I'd write down the passages that mention a known person but match none of my fact templates. Then I'd cluster those leftover sentences by their shape. For example, I'd strip names, IDs and numbers, then count distinct skeletons. I'd read the common skeletons with Read and assign each to a condition or to "distractor". I'd repeat until the unmatched residue is only distractors. This turns "my patterns might be missing things" into a measurable residue that I can shrink.

3. **Do a direct reading pass on a reduced set, not on everyone.** After the residue is clean, the parse should be much more complete. For the remaining uncertainty I'd still not filter by three-of-four. I'd take every person with **at least one `yes`, or any `unknown`, on c2, c3 or c4**. Then I'd print each such person's attached passages from step 1 and read them against all four conditions. If that set is too large to read in full, I'd read the people with the fewest `no` results first.

4. **Handle c1 separately.** Its chain (person, organization, city, coastal) is the likeliest place for gaps. I'd build the organization-to-city and city-to-coastal tables independently, from the organization and city passages, without reference to people. I'd list the organizations and cities that lack an entry and check each by hand, because one missing link silently removes everyone at that organization.

Then I'd intersect as before, re-verify each final candidate against raw passages, and output only `{"matches": [...]}` sorted.

## Added cost

- **Scripts:** the alias index and the skeleton clustering are roughly two more scripts, which is small.
- **Reading:** the residue review is the real cost. I'd expect a few dozen skeleton types to read. The per-person reading pass could mean a few hundred people, or tens of thousands of tokens of passage text.
- **Overall:** I'd estimate this roughly doubles the work versus the original plan, from about 6 steps to about 10 tool-heavy steps. In exchange, I'd have measured coverage, and completeness would no longer rest on my having guessed every phrasing.
- **Remaining risk:** if the residue clustering shows a large share of passages are in unseen formats, I'd tell you that before giving an answer.
