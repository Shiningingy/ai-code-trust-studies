## What a near-miss means here

The query is an intersection of five conditions (c1–c5). A near-miss is a person who satisfies most conditions but fails one or two. I'd define it precisely:

- **Definition:** a person (after entity resolution) for whom my extraction shows at least 3 of the 5 conditions as met, and the rest as unmet or unknown. The 4-of-5 case is the main one, and 3-of-5 is a secondary tier.
- **Why it's useful:** my patterns have gaps, so a qualifying person may be recorded as failing a condition because I missed a phrasing variant. If a person meets four conditions and fails one, the fifth is the place to look for a missed variant.
- **How I'd find them:** I'd parse everything into a per-person table with one column per condition, holding true, false, or unknown. Missing evidence would be marked unknown, not false. Then I'd list everyone with 4 trues, and everyone with 3 trues plus unknowns. For each, I'd grep their name variants across the whole file and read every line that mentions them. For c2 I'd also read the lines for their youth ID, since that evidence sits in a different passage.
- **Classifying each failure:** I'd sort each one into one of three groups.
  - A real failure, such as a past-only or temporary statement, or a count of two events.
  - A missed phrasing.
  - A name-variant split.

## Where near-miss checking breaks down

It only works if the person already looks strong on most conditions. Suppose my patterns miss facts for several of a person's conditions. That person might show only 1 or 2 trues, and no near-miss filter would surface them. The failure is systematic: the same blind spot hides facts for many conditions at once. Examples are an unusual verb ("gave time at"), a different template for guardianship, or a name format my regex doesn't normalize.

## Revised plan: measure the pattern gaps instead of filtering on results

1. **Settle IDs and name resolution first.** This is the same as before. I'd Grep for ID formats and aliases, and build the name-to-ID map before I extract anything.

2. **Find the sentence templates directly.** I'd stop assuming which phrasings exist.
   - I'd use Bash with Python to split every passage into sentences and normalize each one. Names, youth IDs, event IDs, and years would become placeholders, and the foods and courses would stay as they are.
   - I'd count the distinct templates. The data looks generated, so there should be a few hundred at most.
   - I'd read the full template list, with counts, and assign each one a relation or mark it "irrelevant". Anything related to volunteering, guardianship, evening courses, travel preference, food, or dairy would be labeled by hand.
   - This replaces regex guessing with a complete inventory. Any sentence whose template I haven't labeled would show up as an unlabeled row.

3. **Check coverage.** I'd report the share of sentences covered by my labeled templates. I'd also grep for keywords such as `volunt`, `guardian`, `evening`, `train`, `plane`, `dairy`, `milk`, `cheese`, `eat`, and `avoid`, and confirm that every hit falls into a labeled template. Any leftover hit would be a gap.

4. **Extract with the labeled templates.** The script would produce the per-person table, with qualifiers kept (this-year, past, temporary, negated).

5. **Run the near-miss pass on the better table.** It's now a second check, not the only one.

6. **Add an independent check from the other direction.** I'd pick a sample of people at random, including some with few extracted facts. For each I'd read all their raw lines and compare them to the table. If the table and the raw lines disagree anywhere, that points to a pattern gap, and I'd fix it and re-run.

7. **Verify before answering.** I'd re-read the source lines for every final match, then output only the sorted JSON.

## What the revision adds in cost

- **Tool calls:** about 10–20 more Bash and Read calls, mostly for the template inventory, the coverage checks, and the random-sample audit.
- **Reading volume:** a few hundred template lines to label by hand, plus the raw lines for perhaps 20–40 sampled people and every near-miss candidate. That's roughly a few thousand extra lines of context, small compared with the 471 KB file.
- **Time:** about 2–3 times the original plan.
- **What it buys:** the answer no longer depends on my guessing every phrasing in advance. The labeled templates and the coverage report show what was and wasn't captured.
