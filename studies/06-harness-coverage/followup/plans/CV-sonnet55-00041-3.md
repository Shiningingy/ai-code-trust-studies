## What a near-miss means here

A near-miss is a person who satisfies most of c1–c5 under my extraction, but not all. It's a diagnostic. Its main use is to show where my patterns are failing.

**Definition.** For each person, I compute a vector of five booleans (c1..c5) from the extracted facts. The k-miss set is everyone who passes at least 4 of the 5 conditions, so they fail exactly one. I'll also look at people who pass 3, but only as a secondary check.

**How I'd find them.** I'd run the extraction script, build the person × condition table, and sort by the number of conditions passed. Then I'd use Read and Grep on the raw passages for each person who failed exactly one condition. For each, I'd ask whether the failure is real (the passage says something disqualifying) or an artifact (a phrasing my regex missed).

**Why it works.** If my patterns miss one fact for a true match, that person shows up as 4 of 5. The check catches single-pattern errors.

**Where it breaks.** Suppose a qualifying person has facts that my patterns miss for several conditions, say c1 and c4 and c5. They then pass only 2 of 5 and sit far below the near-miss threshold. I would never look at them. Widening the threshold to 3 of 5 or lower would pull in a large share of the population, which defeats the purpose. The check also assumes my misses are independent. They aren't. If a person's passages use an unusual style, such as abbreviated names ("H. Jones") or lowercase, every pattern can fail at once.

## Revised plan

The revision stops relying on my patterns to find candidates. It checks the patterns against the text instead.

1. **Find out what a person ID is.** This is unchanged. I'll Grep for ID patterns and mapping passages.
2. **Build a passage-level inventory first.** I'll write a Python script that splits `context.md` into passages. It will list every distinct sentence template, with names, numbers and events masked out, and count each one. I'll Read that list in full. It should be short, since the passages look templated. This replaces guessing at phrasings with a complete list.
3. **Extract with an explicit "unmatched" bucket.** The script will tag every sentence with a relation, or `UNMATCHED`. I'll iterate on the patterns until `UNMATCHED` holds only irrelevant filler. This is the main safeguard. Coverage is measured by sentences, not by people.
4. **Resolve names before evaluating conditions.** I'll cluster the name variants (abbreviations, case) into canonical people, and I'll log every merge. I'll do this early because a split identity spreads one person's facts across several records, which causes the multi-condition misses described above.
5. **Evaluate c1–c5 and build the table.** This is as before, with the qualifier handling (past, temporary, "genuinely", negations) and the multi-hop guardian → youth → course join.
6. **Run a second, independent check from the opposite direction.** For each condition, I'll Grep loosely, with broad keywords only. Examples are `volunteer`, `guardian`, `train`, `dairy|milk|cheese`, and fruit names. Then I'll compare the sentences those hits cover against the sentences my extractor classified. Any sentence that mentions a keyword but wasn't extracted is a gap. This catches missed facts regardless of how many conditions a person fails.
7. **Sample-read for the multi-miss case.** I'll draw a random sample of people with few extracted facts (0–2 conditions). I'll Read their raw passages in full. If any of them qualify, my coverage is broken, and I go back to step 3. I'll also read every person who is only partly resolved (name variants with unclear merges).
8. **Intersect, sort and report.** I'll still run the near-miss check at 4 of 5, now as a secondary check. I'll report the result with the ambiguities I couldn't settle, and with the coverage figures.
