## What a near-miss means here

A near-miss is someone who almost qualifies on what I parsed. The useful question is why they fell short. If the text says something disqualifying about them, they really fail. If the text says nothing I parsed, I may have missed a fact.

To make that precise, I'll give each person (P-ID) one of three states for each condition:

- **SAT**: I have positive evidence that meets the condition.
- **REFUTED**: I have explicit evidence that disqualifies them. Examples: the only events are from last year, they're an emergency contact rather than a guardian, they like train and plane equally, they chose train only because of a disruption, they're only temporarily skipping cheese, or the youth attends only a daytime course.
- **UNKNOWN**: I found nothing either way.

Some partial results count as UNKNOWN, not REFUTED, because they're one missed fact away from SAT:
- c1 with exactly 2 distinct this-year events.
- c2 where they're a guardian but I can't find the youth's course, or the youth attends an evening course but I can't find a guardian link.
- c4 where they enjoy a food I couldn't classify as fruit or not.

**Near-miss: 4 conditions are SAT and the fifth is UNKNOWN.** Someone who is REFUTED on any condition is not a near-miss. They're excluded, but I'll still spot-check that the refuting fact isn't overridden later (for example, "corrected: actually prefers train").

**How I'd find them:** the Python script (run through Bash) writes a person × condition table to the scratchpad and filters it. For each near-miss, I Grep every alias and P-ID, plus their youth's Y-ID or "Youth NNN" for c2, then Read the matching passages to settle the UNKNOWN by hand.

## When near-miss checking isn't enough

If a qualifying person has missed facts on two or more conditions, they'll look like a 3/5 or 2/5 person and get lost among many genuine non-matches. Making the person filter looser doesn't fix that. The fix is to make sure no text goes unaccounted for, regardless of who it's about. I'd add three checks.

**A. Account for every passage by template (main addition).**
- A script turns each passage into a skeleton. It replaces rostered names and aliases with `<P>`, and P-IDs, Y-IDs, event IDs, course names and foods with placeholders.
- It then groups passages by skeleton. I expect a few dozen to a couple of hundred distinct templates.
- I Read one example of every template, plus every passage that matches none of my regexes.
- Each template gets labelled: which condition it affects, positive or negative, or irrelevant. Any template I hadn't anticipated, such as a new wrapper, a first-person variant, a correction or a negation, goes into the parser before re-running.
- Done means every passage is either parsed by a labelled template or read and judged by me. That guarantees coverage of phrasing, whoever the passage is about.

**B. Account for every name mention.**
- Extract every person-like token: capitalised name pairs, initial forms like `R. Chen`, lowercase variants, P-IDs and Y-IDs.
- Every token must either resolve to exactly one roster entry or go on an "unresolved" list.
- I'll Read every passage on that list. An unlinked alias is the most likely way one person's facts end up spread over several identities, and that would cause misses across several conditions at once.
- Ambiguous initials stay unattributed unless the context resolves them. I'll note any that would have changed the answer.

**C. Full dossiers for everyone with positive evidence on the most selective conditions.**
- Probably c2 and c5 are the most selective; I'll check the counts.
- For everyone SAT on those, the script gathers every passage mentioning any of their aliases, their P-ID or their youth into one file, and I read it in full.
- I'll also read dossiers for anyone with 3 or more SAT and no REFUTED.
- This also lets me check how contradictions resolve in order (for example, "used to" versus "currently", or a later correction).

**Fallback:** if the unparsed residue after A is large (say more than about 5% of passages, or more than about 100 templates I can't classify cleanly), I'll Read the whole file in chunks of about 2,000 lines (about 5 calls) and tag relevant sentences by hand. The script results then become a cross-check, not the primary source.

## Revised plan

1. **Aliases.** Build the roster alias table as before, then run check B to list unresolved mentions and resolve or flag them.
2. **Templates.** Run check A to mine templates, Read one example of each plus the unmatched residue, and update the regexes. Repeat until the residue is empty or fully judged.
3. **Extraction.** Extract facts into the tri-state table, using distinct this-year events, the Y-ID ↔ Youth NNN join, and the evening, current and genuine-preference filters.
4. **Candidates.** Keep everyone with all five SAT. Review near-misses (4 SAT + 1 UNKNOWN) by Grep and Read.
5. **Dossiers.** Run check C: read in full the dossiers of all 5/5 candidates, all near-misses, everyone SAT on the selective conditions, and everyone with 3 or more SAT and no REFUTED.
6. **Consistency checks.**
   - Each final P-ID has its own supporting passage IDs for all five conditions.
   - No final P-ID depends on an ambiguous initial.
   - Re-running the script after any parser change produces the same set.
7. **Output.** Deduplicate the P-IDs, sort them as strings, and return exactly `{"matches": [...]}`.
