## What a near-miss means here

The query asks for the intersection of five conditions. For each person (P-ID), I'd record a status for each condition:

- **PASS:** there's evidence that meets the condition.
- **FAIL-explicit:** there's evidence that rules them out, such as "dislikes eating", "used to attend", "last-year", "emergency contact, but not a guardian" or "no longer prefers".
- **NO-EVIDENCE:** nothing my patterns linked to this person for this condition.

**Definition.** A near-miss is a roster person with 4 PASS and 1 non-PASS. For c1, the threshold condition, it also covers someone who passes the other four and is exactly one distinct this-year event short.

The two kinds of failure need different handling:

- **NO-EVIDENCE near-misses are the dangerous ones.** They point to a sentence my regex missed. I'd grep every alias of the person for wording linked to the failing condition, then read every passage that mentions them.
- **FAIL-explicit near-misses** only need a check that the ruling-out sentence really belongs to this person and isn't another person's sentence or an initial that matches two people.

**How I'd find them.** I'd build a 160 × 5 status matrix with a script in the scratchpad, sort by PASS count, and list everyone at 4/5. I'd also list everyone at 3/5 where both non-PASS cells are NO-EVIDENCE. That's cheap, and it covers part of the problem in your next question.

## When a qualifying person misses several conditions

If my patterns miss a person's facts for two or more conditions, that person sits at 3/5 or lower and looks like an ordinary non-match. A near-miss check built on the same patterns can't catch this, because the error is in the patterns, not in the person. So I'd stop relying on "find what matches" and add two checks that don't depend on my patterns being right.

**1. Coverage accounting (organized by sentence).** I'd split the whole file into sentences and give every one a label:
- a roster sentence,
- a condition pattern it matched (positive or distractor), or
- a known irrelevant template (noise).

Anything left unlabeled is residue. I'd then grep the residue for a broad, data-driven keyword list: train, rail, plane, fly/flight, guardian, ward, youth, `Y\d{3}`, course/class/attend, volunteer, event, every food word found in the file, dairy, lactose, cheese, milk, butter, yogurt, avoid, skip. I'd also make the list bigger by checking word frequencies across the corpus for terms I didn't predict. Every residue sentence containing such a keyword gets read by hand, and either a pattern is fixed or the sentence is classed as noise. The process stops when the condition-keyword residue is empty. This targets missed wording.

**2. Person dossiers (organized by person).** For each of the 160 people, I'd pull every sentence that contains any alias: full name, initials, lowercase forms, possessives, and forms inside quotes. Then I'd check two things:
- Any sentence with a capitalized name or `X. Surname` token that doesn't resolve to a roster alias gets listed. These are unlinked mentions, which point to missing aliases or names that match two people.
- For anyone who still has NO-EVIDENCE on two or more conditions after the fixes, I'd read the whole dossier with Read.

This targets missed links between names and facts. Together the two checks mean a fact is lost only if its sentence avoids every keyword *and* its name avoids every alias form, and the unlinked-name list exists to catch the second case.

## Revised plan

1. **Roster** (Bash: grep/awk; I'll check for python first and fall back to awk): build an alias → P-ID table with all variant forms generated. Flag collisions, meaning one initial for several people or one name with several IDs.
2. **Sentence split and labeling** (Bash script, output to scratchpad): one sentence per line, tagged with passage ID, resolved person(s) and label.
3. **Condition extraction**, as in the original plan (c1: distinct this-year events; c2: guardian → youth with Y/Youth IDs normalized → current evening course; c3–c5 with distractors excluded). The fruit and "temporarily skipping" decisions are made once from the file's own evidence and applied to everyone.
4. **Coverage residue loop** (Grep/Bash, then Read the residue): repeat until no condition-keyword sentence is left unexplained.
5. **Unlinked-name audit:** every name-like token must resolve to a P-ID or be explained.
6. **Status matrix:** 160 × 5, with PASS / FAIL-explicit / NO-EVIDENCE plus the c1 count.
7. **Near-miss and low-evidence review:**
   - everyone at 4/5,
   - c1 counts one below the threshold,
   - anyone with two or more NO-EVIDENCE cells: read the full dossier,
   - every final candidate: re-read all their passages for later contradictions ("no longer", "used to").
8. **Output** `{"matches": [...]}` with IDs sorted lexicographically. I'd only answer once the residue is empty and every matrix cell has a stated reason.

## Rough added cost

- **Tool calls:** about 10–20 extra Bash/Grep calls for the sentence labeling, residue loop and alias audit.
- **Reading:** the residue and dossiers likely come to a few hundred sentences, roughly 20–50k extra tokens. That's well under a full read of the 470 KB file (about 120k tokens). If the residue turns out large, the file is small enough that reading the condition-keyword sentences in full is still affordable.
- **Overall:** roughly 1.5–2× the original effort. That's worth it given there's one scored attempt with exact match.
