## What a near-miss means here

A near-miss is someone my extraction says fails the query, but only barely. If my patterns dropped one fact, that person could actually qualify. I'd define it as a roster person (P-ID) who meets any of these:

1. **Passes four of five conditions** under the strict patterns.
2. **Passes all five except c1, with exactly 2 distinct this-year events.** One more event phrasing would put them at 3.
3. **Fails a condition only because of an exclusion.** For example, their only train line was read as the "disruption" decoy, their only guardian link is to a youth whose course status I couldn't resolve, or their only dairy line is "temporarily skipping". If I read that line wrong, they pass.
4. **Has any condition decided by an ambiguous alias or an unresolved "I"/pronoun.**

To find them, the Python script (run through Bash, output saved to the scratchpad) builds a 160 × 5 matrix. Each cell holds pass, fail, or excluded-by-decoy, plus the IDs of the passages behind it. A filter then lists everyone matching rules 1–4. I'd use Read/Grep on their passages to confirm or overturn each failing cell.

## Why that isn't enough

A near-miss check only works if the patterns fail at most once per person. Suppose a qualifying person's train fact and dairy fact both use a wording I didn't anticipate, and their child's evening course is stated in an odd way. They'd score 2/5 and look like a clear non-match. So the check is still only as good as the patterns. I'd stop asking "did my patterns find this person?" and instead make sure every relevant line in the file is accounted for, whatever any pattern matched.

## Revised plan

**Step 1: Name map (as before), plus a check for leftover names.**
- After building the alias map, extract every capitalized name and every initial-plus-surname form in the file. List any that don't resolve to a roster person.
- Grep and read the leftovers. They may be typos, nicknames or name-order variants of roster people, or genuinely non-rostered decoys.
- Also resolve pronouns ("she", "they") and quoted "I" to the speaker, or to the passage's only named person. If there's more than one candidate, log it as unresolved and don't guess.

**Step 2: Tag every line for each condition with broad, recall-first keywords.** These are much wider than the strict patterns:
- c1: `event|volunteer`
- c2: `guardian|youth|\by\d{3}\b|evening_|daytime_|attend|course`
- c3: `train|plane|rail|fly|flight`
- c4: every fruit name plus `eat|fruit`
- c5: `dairy|milk|cheese|butter|yogurt|lactose|vegan`

The script then checks that every tagged line falls into one bucket:
- (a) matched by a strict positive pattern,
- (b) matched by a known decoy pattern, or
- (c) residual.

I'd read every residual line in chunks, using Read on a scratchpad file the script writes, and hand-label it positive, negative or irrelevant, along with the person it's about. The labels go in a scratchpad file that the script merges back in. No tagged line can stay unclassified, so a person can't be lost because several of their facts used unusual wording.

**Step 3: Settle c2 one youth at a time.** c2 needs two linked facts, so one missed attendance wording breaks it for every guardian of that child.
- Normalize all youth IDs.
- For each youth, collect every line mentioning them and decide by hand whether they currently attend an evening course. Watch for corrections like "switched to", "no longer", "only in the past".
- Join that to the guardian links separately. This means reading up to about 100–200 short youth summaries.

**Step 4: Two-tier candidate selection.**
- *Loose tier (recall):* anyone with at least one broad-keyword line, positive or residual, for all five conditions, and at least 3 distinct event IDs of any kind. This is a superset of the true answer.
- *Strict tier (precision):* for each loose candidate, Grep all of their aliases, read every passage that mentions them, and fill in a per-condition verdict with the supporting passage ID. Later corrections override earlier statements.
- People who drop out of the loose tier fail a condition on keywords alone, so no unusual wording can rescue them. Any such case would also have shown up as a residual in Step 2.

**Step 5: Final checks.**
- Run the near-miss filter from above on the final matrix. Confirm that none of the people it lists were decided by an ambiguous alias.
- Decide tie-break rules before looking at the result: an unresolved attribution doesn't count, and a contradiction is resolved by the later or more explicit statement.
- Output `{"matches": [...]}` sorted as strings, with nothing else.

**Fallback.** If there are more than about 300 residuals, the patterns are clearly poor. In that case I'd read the whole file in Read chunks of about 200 passages and label it directly. That's about 2,300 short passages, roughly 100k tokens.

## Added cost

The original plan was about 10–15 tool calls: a few scripts plus checks on the final candidates. The revision adds:
- **Residual reading:** probably a few hundred lines, about 5–10 Read calls.
- **Youth course review:** about 3–5 calls.
- **Strict review of the loose tier:** maybe 20–40 people at about 10–20 passages each, so 15–30 Grep/Read calls.

That's about 2–3 times the original tool calls and tokens, roughly 30–50 calls in total. The full-read fallback would add about 100k tokens more. Since the answer is scored once by exact match, I think that cost is worth it.
