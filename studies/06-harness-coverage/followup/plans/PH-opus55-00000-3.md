## What the file contains

- `context.md` holds about 2,450 short "Passage D-xxxxx" records followed by the Final Query. The query asks for the person IDs of everyone who (c1) works for an organization based in a coastal city, (c2) ordinarily travels by public transport, and (c3) currently works a night shift.
- **Identities:** 153 roster sentences link full names to IDs, e.g. "The community roster records Priya Gupta under identifier P-036 and also uses the familiar names P. Gupta." Elsewhere people appear as full names, initials, or lowercase forms like "elena Jones" or "samir haddad", and sometimes as "he".
- **Facts and qualifiers:**
  - Shifts appear as "works the night shift", "works the night shift in a former role" (54 times) and "works the day shift".
  - Travel appears as "routinely travels by bus/train/tram/subway". The tricky versions are "...but no longer does", "used the bus once" and "plans to use the train in the future".
  - Employment is "currently works for Org 023", with the org written as Org, Organization, org or organization.
  - Each organization's city is stated once in 32 "Org N is based in <city>" sentences. I found no conflicting cities.
- **Coastal is never defined in the file.** The cities are Halifax, Lunenburg, St. John's, Vancouver, Victoria, Calgary, Edmonton, Ottawa, Regina and Winnipeg, so I'd have to use general knowledge: the first five are coastal and the last five aren't.

## Plan

1. **Build the ID map.** Use Grep to pull all roster sentences and map each full name and familiar name to its P-ID, matching case-insensitively. I'd check whether any familiar name like "P. Jones" belongs to more than one ID. If it does, I'd treat it as ambiguous and use context to decide, not just take the first match.
2. **Map organizations to cities.** Use Grep to extract every "is based in" sentence, convert Org/Organization/org to a single number (so "Org 7" and "Organization 007" match), and label each city coastal or not.
3. **Pull the three kinds of facts.** Using Grep with enough surrounding text, collect every employment, travel and shift sentence along with its passage ID and the person it names, then resolve that person to a P-ID.
4. **Apply the time and habit qualifiers strictly:**
   - c3 counts only "works the night shift" with no "in a former role" (or similar) wording. Day shift, past tense or former-role mentions don't count.
   - c2 counts only habitual travel by bus, train, tram or subway. "No longer does", "used ... once" and "plans to ... in the future" don't count.
   - c1 counts only current employment ("currently works for", or a plain present-tense "works for"), at an org whose city is coastal.
5. **Intersect.** A person qualifies only if all three facts resolve to the same P-ID. I'd put this in a small script (Bash with grep/awk, or Python if it's available) saved in the scratchpad, so I'm not checking 2,400 passages by eye.
6. **Check the borderline cases by hand.** For each match and near-miss, use Read on the source passages to confirm the subject is right.
7. **Output** a single `{"matches": [...]}` with the IDs sorted lexicographically.

## What could make me miss a qualifying person

- **Sentence splitting on "St. John's".** A naive split on "." cuts it to "St.", which loses the city (my own first survey did this). Initials like "O. Tran" break naive splitting the same way.
- **Long, list-style sentences.** Facts can be buried mid-sentence, e.g. "...Org 008 is based in Ottawa, Hana Patel recommended...", or joined by "while", "and" or "but". I need to match on clauses, not just sentence starts.
- **Name variants:** lowercase forms, initials, org written four ways, and "he" pointing back to an earlier name.
- **Wording I haven't seen yet.** I've only surveyed common phrasings, so I'd search for other transport or employment wording (e.g. "commutes", "employed by") before trusting the counts.

## What could make me include a wrong person

- Counting "in a former role", "no longer does", "once" or "in the future" as current habits.
- Mistaking someone else in the same sentence for the subject, e.g. "X works the night shift, while O. Y...".
- Merging two different people who share a surname, or who share an initial-based nickname.
- Treating inland cities (Calgary, Edmonton, Ottawa, Regina, Winnipeg) as coastal, or counting someone who works for an org with no stated city.
- Counting a person with no roster ID. I'd leave them out, since the query asks for person-ID strings.
