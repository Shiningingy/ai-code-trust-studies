## What I found

`context.md` is about 469 KB and 9,493 lines. It holds roughly 2,370 short passages, each headed `### Passage D-xxxxx`. The last section, `### Final Query`, asks for every person who meets four conditions:

- **c1:** works for an organization based in a coastal city.
- **c2:** volunteered at 3 or more distinct events this year.
- **c3:** currently chooses to avoid dairy.
- **c4:** ordinarily travels by public transport.

The output is a JSON object `{"matches": [...]}` with person-ID strings (like `P-097`) sorted lexicographically.

The passages are noisy:

- People appear under varying names, such as "Quinn Reed", "Q. Reed" and "q. Ortiz".
- Some passages link names to IDs, for example "records Quinn Reed under identifier P-097".
- Facts are hedged or time-limited, such as "only in the past", "some time ago", "previously" and "currently".
- Events are written as `event-P-020-0 during this-year` or `old-event-P-075 during last-year`.

## Plan

1. **Read the unseen parts.** I'd read the file in chunks with Read to see every phrasing variant. That covers the alias and ID-linking passages, and how the organization, city, diet and transport facts are worded. I wouldn't rely on the head and tail alone.

2. **Find the phrasings with Grep.** I'd search for each concept (`identifier`, `P-\d+`, `Organization`, `coastal`, `based in`, `dairy`, `public transport`, `volunteered`, `event-`) with `-o` and counts. This shows the full set of templates, including negations and past-tense forms.

3. **Build an alias-to-ID map.** Facts are attached to names, but the answer needs IDs. I'd extract every passage that ties a name or alias to a `P-xxx` ID, using a script in the scratchpad directory (Bash with Python or grep). Then I'd check for collisions, where one short name like "B. Novak" could belong to several IDs. Ambiguous names would be excluded, not guessed.

4. **Resolve each condition separately with regexes.**
   - **c1:** two hops. I need person → organization, then organization → city, and then city → coastal. The city and coastal facts may sit in other passages, so I'd build an organization table first.
   - **c2:** collect `event-P-…` mentions marked `this-year`. I'd count distinct event IDs per person, excluding `last-year` and `old-event` entries and de-duplicating repeats.
   - **c3:** keep only "currently chooses to avoid dairy". I'd exclude past, former or "no longer" wording.
   - **c4:** keep only "ordinarily" or "usually" public-transport use. I'd exclude "occasionally", "once" or negated forms.

5. **Intersect the four sets.** I'd do this by person ID, after alias resolution, and output the IDs sorted lexicographically.

6. **Verify.** I'd print the supporting passage lines for each candidate and check every condition by eye. I'd also look at near-misses, people who satisfy three conditions or fail only on an ambiguous alias or a temporal qualifier.

## What could make me miss a qualifying person

- A fact is stated under an alias or initial form that I fail to link to the ID.
- A condition uses a paraphrase my regex doesn't cover, such as "rides the bus" or "commutes by train".
- The coastal status of a city is stated in a passage far from the organization.
- Event IDs are written in an inconsistent format.

## What could make me include a wrong one

- Two people share a short name like "B. Novak", so I merge them.
- Stale facts ("only in the past", "previously", "last-year") are counted as current.
- Duplicate event mentions inflate the count.
- A negation ("does not ordinarily use public transport") is read as a positive.
- A distractor passage describes another person in a similar way.

I'd stop at the verification step and report the result, without guessing at ambiguous cases.
