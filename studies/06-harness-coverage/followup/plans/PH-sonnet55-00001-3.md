4. **Resolve each condition with explicit rules.**

5. **Intersect and verify.** I'd intersect the four sets. For each candidate, I'd grep the raw passages by ID and by every alias, then read them to check all four facts directly. I'd also spot-check near-misses, meaning people who satisfy three conditions. That tests whether I wrongly excluded someone because of a missed alias or a phrasing variant.

6. **Output.** I'd return only `{"matches": [...]}` with the IDs sorted lexicographically.

## What could make me miss or wrongly include someone
- **Missed alias or paraphrase.** If I match only the templates I've already seen, I'll drop people whose facts are worded differently. Step 1 is meant to reduce this.
- **Ambiguous short names.** An initial plus surname can fit more than one person. Merging two people could wrongly pool their event counts, and failing to merge could split one person's three events into fewer than three.
- **Temporal traps.** Past-only statements, `last-year` events and "previously" can all look like current facts to a naive match.
- **Negation or reversal.** For example, "does not avoid dairy", or a person who used to avoid it and no longer does.
- **Multi-hop gaps.** An organization may have no listed city, or the city's coastal status may be unstated. I'd treat that as unknown and exclude it, and I'd note that assumption.
- **Duplicate events.** The same event ID may appear twice for one person, and I need to count it once.
- **Contradictory passages.** If two passages disagree, I'd need a stated rule, such as the latest or the most explicit statement. I'd report the conflict, not silently pick one.
- **Parser errors.** A regex can silently skip a sentence, so I'd cross-check its counts against the raw grep counts from step 1.
