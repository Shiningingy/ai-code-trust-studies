# Revised plan: catching a person whose facts my patterns mostly miss

## Why the old check fails
The old step 6 only re-read people my patterns had already scored on two of three conditions. If unusual wording hides two of a person's facts, that person scores one or zero, and nothing looks at them again. The fix is to stop using my patterns to decide which sentences get re-read. Instead, every sentence has to be accounted for, and every sentence that mentions one of the three topics gets checked whatever its wording.

## Changes to the plan
Steps 1–5 stay the same. Step 6 is replaced by the following.

**6a. Account for every sentence (Bash: awk/sed/sort/uniq, output saved to the scratchpad).**
- Each sentence produced in step 2 gets one label: **consumed** (a step 3 pattern took a fact from it), **known distractor** or **unclassified**.
- To find the distractors, I'll turn each sentence into a template by replacing roster names, aliases, "Org NNN" forms and city names with placeholders. Then I'll count the templates with `sort | uniq -c`. The file looks machine-generated, so I expect somewhere between a few dozen and a few hundred templates covering about 2,448 passages.
- I'll read the whole template list once and mark each template as one of the three conditions (current or qualified), a roster entry or a distractor.
- Any template I didn't plan for becomes a new pattern, and I rerun steps 3–5.
- This catches odd wording no matter how many of a person's conditions it affects, because whole sentence types are checked, not people.

**6b. Sweep for topic words, each condition separately (Grep with `-i`, content mode, `-C 1`).**
- I'll search with a wide word list:
  - **Transport:** commute, bus, train, tram, subway, metro, ferry, transit, streetcar, light rail, pass
  - **Shift:** night, overnight, graveyard, late shift, evening shift, after dark
  - **Employment:** employ, staff, job, hired, joined, works at, position, Organization, org
- Every hit must match a sentence labelled consumed in 6a. If it doesn't, I read it.
- This works one condition at a time, so it doesn't matter how many conditions a person is short on.

**6c. Check each person's full set of mentions.**
- For each of the 155 rostered people, a Bash loop collects every sentence that mentions any of their name forms, ignoring case.
- I list any person who has a mention left over after 6a or 6b. I also list anyone missing a value for one or more conditions while having sentences on that topic that weren't consumed.
- I'll read those people's passages with Grep or Read at the right offset.
- This is also where I resolve pronoun sentences ("He works…", "She routinely…"): an unclassified sentence with no name gets linked to the person named in the sentence before it, and I check it by reading.

**6d. Check the organization-to-city side.**
- Every organization someone works for must have a city. Every "based in" sentence must have been parsed.
- If an organization has no city, I Grep its number in all its spellings ("Org 013", "Organization 013", "org 013", "013").

**6e. Run the intersection again** (step 5) on the corrected facts. Then do the existing by-hand check on every final candidate.

## Extra cost
- **Bash work:** about 5–10 more script runs (templates, labels, sweep, per-person loop). That costs little.
- **Reading:** the template list (maybe 100–300 lines) plus whatever 6a–6c leave unexplained. I expect roughly 20–50k extra tokens, compared with about 120k or more to read the whole 473 KB file.
- **Worst case:** if there turn out to be many templates because the generator varies wording a lot, the leftover sentences grow. The fallback is to read every topic sentence for all 155 people, about 15 sentences each. That costs nearly as much as reading the file, but nothing would be missed.
- **What I get:** every sentence about transport, shifts or employment is either matched by a pattern I've checked or read by me. So a qualifying person can't be dropped just because their sentences are worded unusually, however many conditions that affects.

Output format doesn't change: only `{"matches": [...]}`.
