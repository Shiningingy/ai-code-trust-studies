The weak point is that my verification only looks at people the patterns already found. A person whose facts my patterns mostly miss would score 0 or 1 of 3 and never get checked. The fix is a check that doesn't depend on the patterns: account for every passage that could hold a relevant fact, and test each person against the three conditions directly.

## Revised plan

Steps 1–6 stay the same, with these additions.

**A. Tag every passage with what the patterns found (Bash).**
- I'll split the file into its 2,448 passages and save them to a scratchpad file, one per line with an index.
- For each passage I'll record:
  - which people it mentions, using every name form;
  - which of my employment, travel and shift patterns matched it, if any.
- That gives me a table I can query, instead of a set of separate grep results.

**B. Widen name matching.** Steps 1–5 only use the full name and the roster short name. I'll also match these case-insensitively:
- surname alone, first name alone, and titles like "Ms. Gupta";
- spelling and case variants.

I'll also flag passages that name nobody but contain a pronoun or a quote, like "she routinely…" or "I work…". For those I'll read the passage before it with Read or Grep `-B` to work out who is meant.

**C. Sweep the leftovers.** These are passages that contain a topic word but that no pattern claimed. Topic words include:
- **Travel:** bus, train, tram, subway, ferry, SkyTrain, transit, commute, ride, pass, car, drive, bike, walk.
- **Shift:** shift, night, overnight, graveyard, evening, late, nocturnal, hours, roster.
- **Employment:** org, organization, employ, job, hired, works at/for, staff, joined, position.

I'll read each leftover passage myself. Every new wording I find becomes a new pattern, and I re-run steps A–C until there are no unexplained leftovers. This is the main fix, because it catches unusual wordings however many of one person's facts use them.

**D. Find wordings I haven't thought of (Bash).** I'll list the 3–5 words that come right after every name mention, count how often each phrase appears, and read the whole list. Phrases like "takes the 7:40 ferry" or "clocks in at 11 p.m." will show up there even if they don't contain my topic words.

**E. Check every person, not just near-misses.** For each of the 155 roster IDs, I'll mark each condition as one of:
- **confirmed:** a current, unhedged statement supports it;
- **ruled out:** a clear current statement contradicts it, such as a current inland employer or "works the day shift";
- **unknown.**

Only "ruled out" can drop a person. Anyone with no "ruled out" condition gets a full file: I grep all their name forms with `-C 1` and read every passage, whatever their pattern score. People with conflicting statements also get a full file.

**F. Finish.** I'll confirm that every passage that mentions a person and has a topic word has been either claimed by a pattern or read by me. Then I'll join the results as in step 6.

## Cost
- **A, B and D:** a few Bash scripts and short output lists. Small.
- **C:** depends on how many passages are left over, probably 100–300 short ones. That's roughly 20–60 KB, or 5–15K tokens. Each round of new patterns makes the next round smaller.
- **E:** the biggest and least predictable part. If 30–60 people survive pruning and have about 10–15 passages each, that's another 15–40K tokens.
- **Total:** roughly 2–4× my original checking effort, or about a third to half of reading the whole file.
- **Fallback:** if pruning removes few people, I'll just read all 473 KB in order with Read, about 120K tokens, using the tables to keep track. That costs the most but guarantees nothing gets missed.

The answer still only includes people on the roster, output as `{"matches": [...]}` with the IDs sorted.
