# Revised plan: catching a person whose facts my patterns miss on two or more conditions

## Why the old check fails

My earlier plan found candidates with patterns and then re-read the people who were one condition short. Suppose a qualifying person's employment and travel are both worded in ways I didn't anticipate, like "is on staff at Organization 012" and "gets to work on the streetcar". My patterns would credit them with at most one condition. They'd never become a near-miss, and nothing would make me look at them again.

The fix is to stop relying on the patterns to decide what I read. Instead, every clause in the file must either be explained by a pattern or be read by me. A missed person then shows up as unexplained text, however many of their conditions were missed.

## Revised plan

1. **Split the file into clauses.** Use a Bash script saved in the scratchpad. I'll run `python --version` first and use Python if it's there, otherwise awk. Before splitting, the script protects "St. John's" and initials like "O. Tran". It then splits on sentence ends and on joiners like "while", ", and", "but" and ";". Each clause keeps its passage ID.

2. **Sort every clause into one of four buckets** using the patterns from my original plan:
   - roster sentence
   - org–city sentence
   - fact clause (employment, travel or shift, including the qualified versions such as former role, no longer, once and in the future)
   - known filler, i.e. wording I've confirmed is irrelevant

   Anything that fits none of these goes into a **residue file**. This is the key change.

3. **Catch wording that only looks like a fact.** A loose keyword list runs over the fact clauses too:
   - shift: night, overnight, evening, late, graveyard, shift, rota
   - travel: bus, train, tram, subway, metro, transit, rail, streetcar, ferry, commute, ride, transport
   - employment: works for, employed, staff, job, hired, joined, employer, Org/Organization

   If a clause hits a keyword but no strict pattern, or hits a strict pattern plus an unexpected modifier (e.g. "used to", "sometimes", "until last year"), it goes to the residue too.

4. **Read the residue and improve the patterns.** I go through the residue file with Read. When I see a new way of saying something, I add a pattern and rerun the script. I repeat until the residue holds only clauses I've read and confirmed are irrelevant. Even a person whose conditions are all worded in new ways must surface here.

5. **Check each person's mentions.** For all 153 roster IDs, the script gathers every clause that mentions any form of their name, whether full name, familiar name, lowercase form or initials. It also includes "he/she/they" clauses, linked to the most recent name in the same passage. If any of those clauses is unclassified, I read the whole passage. This catches facts that are attached to a person through a pronoun or a name variant my map missed. Separately, I grep for capitalised name-like tokens that aren't in the map, to catch name variants I haven't seen.

6. **Spot-check the classifier.** I read about 40 randomly chosen passages in full and compare my own reading with the script's labels. If any fact was missed, I fix the pattern and rerun. This gives me a direct estimate of how often the script misses facts, rather than assuming it doesn't.

7. **Then continue as before.** Apply the strict qualifier rules, intersect the three conditions on the P-ID, hand-check every match and every person meeting at least two conditions, and output the sorted `{"matches": [...]}`.

## Rough added cost

- **Script:** about 60–80 more lines for clause splitting, residue tracking and per-person dossiers. That's 2–4 extra Bash runs as the patterns improve.
- **Reading:** this is the main cost. If the patterns cover about 90% of clauses, the residue is a few hundred clauses, roughly 15–30k tokens across maybe 5–10 Read calls. The 40-passage spot check adds about 3–5k tokens. The per-person passage reads add some more, depending on how many people have unclassified mentions.
- **Overall:** about 1.5–2× the original plan's effort.

## What could still slip through

A fact worded so oddly that it matches none of the patterns or keywords, and that also lands in a clause I misread as filler during step 4. The spot check in step 6 is there to show whether that is happening at a meaningful rate.
