I haven't evaluated anyone against the conditions yet. The revised plan below replaces step 6 and adds two new steps.

## The flaw in the old plan

Step 6 only re-read people who passed three conditions. If my patterns missed two of a real match's conditions, that person would show as passing two. They'd never be re-read and would be silently dropped. The fix is to stop choosing whom to review by how many conditions they pass. Instead, review is driven by **why** each person fails.

## Revised plan

**Steps 1–5 stay the same** (parse, identity table, phrasing inventory, extraction, name resolution). The one change is that every (person, condition) pair gets one of three labels instead of pass/fail:
- **pass**: a sentence shows the condition is met.
- **explicit fail**: a sentence actually contradicts it. For example, the person currently works for an organization based in Calgary, or "travels by bus, but no longer does", or "no longer avoids dairy".
- **no evidence**: no sentence the patterns recognize. c2 with fewer than three this-year events always counts as "no evidence", because a missed phrasing could cause an undercount.

**New step A: audit sentences that matched no pattern (does not depend on any person).**
- A Python script (via Bash, in the scratchpad) marks each sentence as matched by some extraction pattern, a roster line, or unmatched.
- For unmatched sentences, it swaps names, P-IDs, org numbers, event IDs and cities for placeholders. It then groups identical templates with counts, like `uniq -c`.
- I read **every** unmatched template, not just keyword hits. The file looks machine-generated, so 2,370 passages should come down to a few hundred templates or fewer.
- Any template that expresses a condition goes into the patterns, and steps 4–5 run again. This catches paraphrases like "takes the metro daily" or "is employed by" for everyone at once, however many conditions they affect.
- The same script lists capitalized name-like tokens that don't resolve to a roster ID. I check those with Grep for missed aliases.

**New step B: every exclusion needs a reason I've read.**
- A person can be excluded only if at least one condition is an **explicit fail**, and I've confirmed that sentence by reading it (Grep for the name with `-C 2`). That's at most one short check per excluded person, about 157 checks.
- If someone has no explicit fail (every failing condition is "no evidence"), I build a dossier from every sentence that mentions any of their name forms, case-insensitive, including initials. I read the full dossier, written to the scratchpad, using Read.
- So a real match whose facts are worded oddly can't drop out through missing evidence, however many conditions the patterns missed.

**Step 6 (revised): confirm the matches.** I re-read the source sentences for every proposed match, as before: polarity, timing, distinct this-year events, and the org → city → coastal chain.

**Step 7 is unchanged:** output `{"matches": [...]}`.

## Added cost
- **Step A:** one extra script, plus reading the template list. Probably 20–60 KB of text, a few Read calls. Each new pattern it finds means one cheap rerun of steps 4–5.
- **Step B, explicit-fail checks:** up to about 157 short Grep lookups. I'd batch them in one script that prints each person's deciding sentence, so it's one to three Read calls.
- **Step B, dossiers:** this cost varies. If, after step A, most people still have a condition with no evidence (c2 is the likeliest), the dossiers add up to most of the file. That's about 470 KB, roughly 120k tokens over about 15–25 Read calls. If most people have an explicit fail, it falls to a small fraction of that.
- **Overall:** about 2–4 times the old plan's reading effort in the worst case. The heavy part only shows up when the patterns are actually missing information.
